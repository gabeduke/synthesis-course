Lesson 14

Three VCOs, one filter

The AKS Mini has three oscillators and a single set of knobs for all of them. Those knobs set what the oscillators sound like; the voice mode decides which notes they play. After them comes one filter and one envelope, shared by everything, and that one fact explains most of how the AKS Mini behaves when you play chords.

Saw and square, mixed

Each oscillator makes a saw and a square at once. Saw and Square are two level knobs, so you mix them rather than choose one, and the mix applies to all three oscillators. Lesson 2 covered why they sound as they do: the saw has every harmonic, the square only the odd ones, so the saw is bright and buzzy and the square hollow.

Shape

Shape changes both waves at once. On the square it’s pulse width: from a thin 10% pulse, through an even square at the middle, to 90%. Lesson 2’s PWM is this knob turned by an LFO. On the saw, the guide says it “reverses saw into ramp”: the slow rise and sudden drop turn into a sudden rise and slow fall. The page passes through a triangle on the way, which is a guess; listen to how yours gets from one to the other.

A 10% pulse and a 90% pulse sound the same. One is the other upside down, and your ears can’t tell which way up a wave is. Turn Shape across the whole range with only Square up and you’ll hear the sound get fuller towards the middle and thinner again past it.

Detune and octave

Detune pulls the oscillators apart, up to a whole tone. On the page it moves oscillator 2 up and oscillator 3 down by the same amount, and oscillator 1 stays put. A few cents gives slow beating, the same effect as lesson 6’s Detune with three voices instead of seven. A semitone or a tone gives a cluster, which can sound like a chord built into every note.

Octave moves all three up or down, three octaves each way on the panel. Over MIDI the guide lists six steps, named the old organ way: 32′ is the lowest and 1′ the highest, and 8′ is normal pitch.

Paraphonic: shared by every key

The Virtual Analog engine in lessons 2 to 10 is polyphonic. Every key gets its own oscillators, filter and envelope: play a chord one note at a time and each note starts its own attack and fades at its own pace.

The AKS Mini is paraphonic. It can play three different pitches, one per oscillator, but they all go through the same filter and the same envelope. That changes how chords behave:

  • One envelope for the chord. Hold two keys, then add a third, and the envelope starts again for all three: the notes you were already holding swell or pluck again. With the power-up option the guide calls retrigger, only the first key after all keys are up starts it (the page calls it First key).
  • One filter for the chord. A filter sweep moves every note together. There’s no per-note brightness from velocity or key tracking.
  • Three keys at most. Hold a fourth and one key goes silent. Note priority picks which: the last three keys you pressed, the lowest three or the highest three. The guide also lists a “nearest” option, which isn’t modelled here.

The Three VCOs picture shows the oscillators sharing out the keys. With one key held, all three play it, so a single note is as full as a chord. Hold more than three and the key that loses is struck through in pink. One thing the page can’t know yet: what the AKS Mini does with two keys held. The page doubles the first key on the spare oscillator. Check on your unit, and listen for whether the first note of a two-note chord is louder.

Unison, Octave and Fifth

The other voice modes play one key at a time, by note priority, and use all three oscillators on it. Unison puts all three on the key, which with a little Detune is the fattest sound the AKS Mini makes. Octave and Fifth add the key an octave or a fifth above it. The guide names these modes but not which oscillator moves; the page moves oscillator 2. Its MIDI chart also lists two ring-modulation modes that don’t appear anywhere on the panel; whether they exist is one more thing to check on your unit.

The filter

EMS’s filter was famous for misbehaving well. EMS rated it at 18 dB per octave. Measurements of the VCS3 circuit by Tim Stinchcombe show a four-pole diode ladder: gentler than that just past the cutoff, and steeper further away. The AKS Mini’s spec sheet lists a 12 and a 24 dB low-pass, without saying how you choose between them, so the page gives you both.

Response is EMS’s word for resonance. Turn it up and a peak rides on the cutoff, as in lesson 3. Turn it all the way and the original stopped filtering and started oscillating: a clean sine at the cutoff frequency, which is why EMS labelled it the filter/oscillator. The guide doesn’t say whether the AKS Mini’s filter does this. The page’s does, near the top of the knob.

With both oscillators down and Response up, you’re playing the filter as an oscillator. Frequency is now its pitch, and EG int turns the envelope into a pitch sweep: the basis of countless EMS laser zaps and bird calls. Response has no MIDI number, so it stays a hands-on knob.

When to reach for it

  • A thick mono lead or bass: Unison, a little Detune, Saw up, Square under it.
  • Moving chords: Poly with a slow Attack. Each new key re-swells the whole chord, which works as a rhythm if you play with it rather than against it.
  • A fixed interval: Fifth mode turns a one-finger line into parallel fifths, an organ or early-synth sound.
  • A whistle, a zap or a bird: oscillators off, Response up, and the envelope or the joystick on Frequency.

On the AKS Mini

  1. Start neutral: Saw up, Square down, Shape at 0, Detune in the middle, Frequency open, Response low.
  2. Hold one key and bring Square up, then take Saw down. Then sweep Shape with only Square up and listen for the fullest point.
  3. Find the voice mode setting (CC 40 in the guide; the guide doesn’t say where it is on the panel) and switch to Unison. Turn Detune slowly away from the middle and count the beats.
  4. Back in Poly, set a slow Attack, hold two keys and add a third. Then hold four keys and find which one drops. Note priority is in the power-up menu: hold FUNC while switching on.
  5. Take Saw and Square down, Response to the top, and sweep Frequency. If you hear a sine, your filter self-oscillates.

Where it comes from

1969
The VCS3’s filter/oscillator uses a ladder of diodes rather than Moog’s patented ladder of transistors. Its slightly unruly character becomes part of the EMS sound.
1975–83
Paraphonic keyboards spread: string machines, and synths like Korg’s Mono/Poly, share one filter and envelope across several pitches because a full voice per key is expensive.
2010s–20s
Paraphony returns on small analog synths, for the same reason: three oscillators cost far less than three complete voices.
