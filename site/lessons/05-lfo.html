Lesson 5

LFOs and modulation

A held synth note with nothing moving sounds like a test tone. Real instruments never hold still: singers add vibrato, organ speakers spin, players lean on notes. An LFO is a third hand that keeps turning a knob for you, steadily, as long as you like.

An oscillator too slow to hear

LFO stands for low-frequency oscillator. It’s an oscillator just like the ones in lesson 2, but it runs far below hearing, from a cycle every several seconds up to a few dozen per second. You don’t listen to it. You point it at another control, and that control moves up and down in its shape.

Where you point it decides what you hear:

  • Pitch: vibrato. The note bends slightly sharp and flat.
  • Loudness: tremolo. The note pulses louder and softer.
  • Filter cutoff: wah, wobble, or a slow sweep. The tone opens and closes.

The pink trace on the left is the LFO itself, scrolling by. Raise LFO to cutoff and the dashed filter line on the spectrum starts sliding in time with it. Raise Pitch depth and the harmonic peaks wobble side to side. Raise Amp depth and the whole spectrum breathes up and down.

Shape and speed

The LFO’s wave shape is the shape of the movement:

  • Triangle and sine: smooth back and forth. Natural vibrato, gentle sweeps.
  • Square: jumps between two values. On pitch, that’s a trill.
  • Saw up and saw down: ramps, then snaps back. Rising or falling zaps and rhythmic sweeps.
  • S&H (sample and hold): a new random value at every step. On the filter, that’s the classic bubbling “computer” sound.

Speed matters as much as shape. Around 5 Hz on pitch sounds like a singer. Under 1 Hz on the filter is a slow, evolving sweep. Fast tremolo starts to sound like a helicopter.

LFO delay

Singers rarely start a note with vibrato. They hit the pitch, hold it, then let the vibrato in. LFO delay does the same: each new note starts steady, and the LFO’s effect fades in afterwards. Set Pitch depth to about 20, Speed around 50, then raise LFO delay and play single notes.

What happens if it speeds up?

Keep raising an LFO’s speed and something strange happens. Past roughly 20 cycles a second, you stop hearing the movement as movement. Vibrato that fast turns into a change of tone, with new, often metallic frequencies. That accident is the basis of FM synthesis, and it’s where lesson 8 begins.

When to reach for it

  • Leads: a little pitch vibrato with some delay.
  • Pads: a very slow LFO on the filter, so a long chord keeps changing.
  • Basses: usually none, or a tempo-feeling filter wobble for a deliberate effect.
  • Sound effects: S&H on the filter, or a fast saw on pitch.

On the FM-1

  1. Press LFO on any preset. The rows are Wave, Speed, Delay, Pitch Mod Depth, Amp Mod Depth, Pitch Sensitivity and LFO Sync. They’re the same on all three engines.
  2. Everything starts at zero depth, so the LFO moves nothing until you raise a depth.
  3. On a Virtual Analog preset, the LFO also reaches the filter through LFO to Cutoff, in the Filter group under EDIT. It’s one of the eight choices you can put on a knob, too.
  4. LFO Sync restarts the wave each time you press a key, so every note’s wobble starts the same way.
  5. If you play the FM-1 from a MIDI keyboard, its mod wheel and aftertouch can push the same LFO harder.

Where it comes from

1936
Hammond adds a chorus generator to its organs, and in the 1940s a scanner vibrato that sweeps the sound through a delay line. Both are mechanical modulation, decades before the word LFO.
1941
Don Leslie starts selling his rotating speaker. The spinning horn makes pitch and loudness swirl, and its two speeds are still copied by synth LFOs today.
1960s
Modular synthesizers make modulation explicit: any oscillator, run slowly, can be patched into any other module’s control input.
