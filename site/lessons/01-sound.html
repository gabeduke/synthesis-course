Lesson 1

Sound you can see

Sound is air being pushed and pulled very quickly. A synthesizer is a machine for deciding exactly how that push and pull goes. Before turning any knobs, it helps to learn the two pictures this course uses everywhere: the waveform and the spectrum.

The waveform: pressure over time

A speaker cone moves forward and back, squeezing and stretching the air in front of it. Draw its position over time and you get a waveform. The oscilloscope on the left of the panel below does exactly that, freezing the picture each time the wave crosses the centre line going up, so a steady note draws as a steady shape.

Three things about that shape matter:

  • How often it repeats is the pitch. Repetitions per second are measured in hertz (Hz). The A above middle C repeats 440 times a second. Double the rate and you go up an octave: 880 Hz is the next A up.
  • How tall it is is the loudness. Bigger swings push more air.
  • What shape it is is the tone, or timbre. A smooth curve sounds pure; a shape with sharp corners and edges sounds bright and buzzy.

Pitch and loudness are easy to hear and easy to see. Tone is the interesting one, and the spectrum on the right is how we’ll read it.

The spectrum: what the tone is made of

In 1822 the mathematician Joseph Fourier published an idea that turns out to underpin all of this: any shape that repeats can be built by adding together sine waves, the smooth, pure kind, at whole-number multiples of the repeat rate. For a note at 110 Hz, those are sines at 110, 220, 330, 440 Hz and up. They’re called harmonics (or partials).

A spectrum shows how much of each frequency is in a sound. Low frequencies are on the left, high on the right, and taller means louder. Hold a note on the panel above and you’ll see a row of peaks: that’s the harmonic ladder. A sine has a single peak. A saw has a peak at every rung, each a little shorter than the last. A square has peaks only at the odd rungs.

The axis is spaced so each octave takes the same width, because that’s how we hear pitch. That’s why the rungs of the ladder crowd together towards the right.

Build a wave by hand

Here’s Fourier’s idea you can touch. Each bar is one sine wave. The first is the note itself; the second is twice its frequency, the third three times, and so on. Drag the bars and the waveform on the right is their sum.

  1. Press Play, then Add them one at a time. Starting from one sine, it adds harmonics at a falling level, and the curve sharpens into a sawtooth. Listen to the tone get brighter with each one.
  2. Choose Square. Every other bar is empty: a square wave uses only the odd harmonics. That gap is why squares sound hollow next to saws.
  3. Choose Triangle. Odd harmonics again, but they fade out much faster, so it sounds soft, almost like a sine with a little edge.
  4. Make something of your own. Pull up only the high bars and the sound gets thin and glassy; keep only the first two or three and it gets round and woody.

Why this matters for everything that follows

  • Filters (lesson 3) work by turning harmonics down. You can only take away what’s there, which is why synths start from bright waves like saws.
  • Detune (lesson 6) makes harmonics beat against each other, and the high ones beat fastest.
  • FM (lesson 8) creates new partials, and some of them don’t sit on the ladder at all. That’s where bells and metallic tones come from.

Where it comes from

1822
Joseph Fourier’s Analytical Theory of Heat shows repeating signals can be written as sums of sine waves. He was studying heat flow, not music.
1863
Hermann von Helmholtz’s On the Sensations of Tone uses tuned glass resonators to pick individual partials out of a sound, and argues that the mix of partials is what we hear as timbre.
1897–1906
Thaddeus Cahill builds the Telharmonium, a 200-ton instrument that makes tones by adding up harmonics from spinning electrical generators. It’s often called the first synthesizer.
