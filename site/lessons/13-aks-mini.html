Lesson 13

The AKS Mini

Behringer’s AKS Mini is a small analog synthesizer with three oscillators, one filter, an envelope made of four times instead of four stages, a joystick and a spring reverb. Its name points back to one of the strangest and most loved instruments of the early 1970s. This lesson is about what it took from that ancestor, what it left behind, and how to play it from the page.

The first twelve lessons were about the FM-1+VA, a digital instrument where every note gets its own oscillators, filter and envelope. The AKS Mini works differently. It has one of everything after the oscillators, shared by every key you hold, and its oscillators are analog: they run all the time, drift a little as they warm up, and never quite sound the same twice. Most of what you’ve learned carries over. The differences are what this short track is for.

What this track is built from

When this was written, Behringer had published only a draft Quick Start Guide for the AKS Mini, marked version 0.0. Its control ranges, power-up menu and MIDI numbers are used here as given, but the guide has visible mistakes (it calls the envelope an “ADSR”, lists the same key for both joystick axes, and its voice-mode numbers overlap), so treat everything on the AKS Mini side as provisional until your own unit confirms it. Where the guide says nothing, the page makes a reasonable choice and says so, and the steps say “check on your unit”.

The page’s AKS Mini is a model built from that guide, for learning the layout. It isn’t a copy of the circuits, and it won’t sound the same.

A briefcase from 1972

In 1969 a small London company, EMS, released the VCS3: a synthesizer in a wooden case the size of a shoebox. A year or two later it went into a briefcase as the Synthi A, and in 1972 the Synthi AKS added a flat touch keyboard and a sequencer in the lid. They were cheap enough, for the time, that bands could take one on the road, and they ended up on records by Pink Floyd, Roxy Music, The Who and many others.

Their sound came from a few unusual parts: a filter that could whistle on its own (EMS labelled it the filter/oscillator), an envelope shaper built around times rather than a held key, a ring modulator, a spring reverb, and a joystick that could move any two things at once. And they had no fixed signal path at all.

The pin matrix

Instead of cables, the VCS3 and Synthi had a 16 by 16 grid of holes. Each row is a source (an oscillator, the noise generator, the filter’s output, the envelope, the joystick); each column is a destination (the filter’s input, an oscillator’s pitch, the reverb, an output channel). Push a pin into a hole and that source feeds that destination. Pins are named by column and row: H3 sends oscillator 1 into the filter.

The matrix is why those instruments sounded so wild: anything could modulate anything, including itself. It’s also the main thing the AKS Mini leaves out.

  • Kept: the trapezoid envelope shaper with its Attack, On, Decay and Off times; a filter with EMS’s name for resonance, Response; the joystick; a spring reverb (digital, on the Mini); a sequencer.
  • Left out: the pin matrix, the noise generator, the input amplifiers for processing other sounds, the separate ring modulator on the panel, and the original’s two output channels with their own filters.
  • Added: three oscillators that all play notes (the original’s third was mostly a slow one, for modulation), paraphonic chords, a separate LFO, MIDI, and a 27-key touch keyboard.

Its signal path

With no matrix, the AKS Mini is wired one way, like the FM-1+VA’s Virtual Analog engine: oscillators into a filter, into an amplifier, into the reverb. The envelope and the LFO move things along the way, and the joystick can move any two knobs.

Three pictures are new. Envelope shaper draws the trapezoid with a dot where it is now; the dashed blue line under it is how far the VCA is open. Three VCOs shows which note each oscillator is playing. Hold one key and all three play it; hold three and each takes one. Lesson 14 is about why that matters.

Connect it

The AKS Mini’s USB-C socket carries MIDI as well as power, and it needs no driver. There’s also a 5-pin DIN MIDI input, but no MIDI output, so the page can play it and set its knobs but can’t read them back.

  • The page looks for a MIDI port with “AKS” in its name. Nobody has reported the real name yet, so if it isn’t found, choose the port from the list yourself.
  • The channel has to match the one the AKS Mini receives on. According to the guide you set it by holding MEMORY/WRITE while switching on, then pressing one of the sixteen keys the guide numbers 32 to 47, for channels 1 to 16. Check on your unit.
  • Every AKS panel then gains Play on, Mirror to AKS Mini and Send settings to AKS Mini, which work as they do for the FM-1.
  • To draw the real thing, plug its headphone output into an audio interface and choose that input under Audio in the connection panel at the top right. Its trace is purple, labelled “Audio in”. Turn the AKS Mini’s volume down first: a headphone output into a line input can be loud.

Playing it from your FM-1

The AKS Mini’s own keys are small touch plates. If you have an FM-1 connected too, the page can pass its keys on, so one instrument plays the other:

  1. Connect the FM-1 from the panel at the top right, and the AKS Mini above.
  2. In the top-right panel, switch on FM-1 keys also play the page’s synth.
  3. Click an AKS panel on this page, so its keyboard is the one listening, and set Play on to AKS Mini (or Both, to hear the model alongside).

The page is in the middle, so it can show you what’s happening: the keys light up, and the Three VCOs picture follows what you play. A pitch wheel, a mod wheel and a sustain pedal come along too, if your keyboard has them; the mod wheel goes out as CC 1.

The SMK-37 as the AKS Mini’s keyboard

Played this way, the SMK-37 makes its own sound as well as the AKS Mini’s. To use it only as a keyboard, set GLOBE › MIDI › Local Control to Off: its keys, arpeggiator and Note Engine then send MIDI without playing its own engine. Set it back to On when you’re done, or the SMK-37 will seem broken next time.

Without a computer: the SMK-37’s 3.5 mm MIDI port is an output and carries the same messages as USB, so a 3.5 mm-to-DIN adapter into the AKS Mini’s MIDI input connects them directly. There are two wiring standards for those adapters (usually called type A and type B); check which one the SMK-37 uses before buying one.

Before you start on the AKS Mini

  1. Give it time to warm up. The guide asks for about fifteen minutes before tuning; analog oscillators drift until they settle.
  2. The guide also describes a calibration routine for the oscillators at power-up. If notes are out of tune with each other after warming up, look for it there.
  3. Set every knob to the middle, Attack and Decay low, Trapez and Signal up, Response down, and play one key. That’s your neutral starting point for the next two lessons.

Where it comes from

1969
Peter Zinovieff, Tristram Cary and David Cockerell’s company EMS releases the VCS3 in London: three oscillators, noise, a ring modulator, a filter that doubles as an oscillator, an envelope shaper, a spring reverb, a joystick, and a pin matrix to connect them.
1971–72
The same circuits go into a briefcase as the Synthi A. The Synthi AKS adds the KS: a flat touch keyboard with a sequencer in the case’s lid.
1973
Pink Floyd build “On the Run”, on The Dark Side of the Moon, around a sequence played on a Synthi AKS. In early Roxy Music, Brian Eno uses EMS synths to treat the rest of the band’s sound.
2026
Behringer’s AKS Mini, first shown as a render in 2022, goes on pre-order: a 3-oscillator paraphonic synth with the EMS envelope shaper and joystick, and without the matrix.
