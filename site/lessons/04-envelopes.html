Lesson 4

Envelopes

A piano note hits hard and fades. A violin can swell in slowly and hold for as long as the bow keeps moving. Same pitch, same rough tone, completely different instruments. The difference is the envelope: how a sound changes from the moment you press a key to well after you let go.

Attack, decay, sustain, release

Almost every synth describes loudness over time with the same four stages, ADSR:

  • Attack: how long the sound takes to rise from silence to full level after you press a key.
  • Decay: how long it then takes to fall to the sustain level.
  • Sustain: the level it holds while you keep the key down. This one is a level, not a time.
  • Release: how long it takes to fade to silence after you let go.

The left picture is the envelope you’ve set. The middle one is the measured loudness of what’s actually coming out, scrolling past. Try these shapes:

  • Organ: Attack 0, Sustain 100, Release low. On and off like a switch.
  • Pluck: Attack 0, Decay around 40, Sustain 0, Release around 30. A sharp hit that dies away even if you hold the key.
  • Pad: Attack around 60, Sustain 80 or more, Release high. It fades in and lingers after you let go.
  • Swell: Attack near the top. Hold a chord and wait for it to bloom.

Notice that Release on the FM-1’s Virtual Analog engine runs from 0.1 to 2 seconds. For longer tails, reverb (lesson 7) takes over.

The filter envelope

An envelope doesn’t have to control loudness. Pointed at the filter’s cutoff, it changes the tone over the life of a note: bright at the start, darker as it fades. That’s how real plucked strings behave, and it’s the core of most synth basses and plucks.

The FM-1’s Virtual Analog filter has its own simple envelope. Every note jumps the cutoff up by the Filter envelope amount, then lets it fall back over Filter decay. On the hardware a third control, Shape, changes the curve of that fall.

Watch the dashed filter line: each note flings it to the right, then it slides back. A big amount with a short decay is a pluck or a “blip”. A long decay with a low cutoff is a slow, wah-like opening. Push resonance up and you’re most of the way to an acid bass.

When to reach for it

  • Notes blurring together? Shorten Release.
  • Pad sounds abrupt? Lengthen Attack and Release.
  • Bass lacks punch? Keep Attack at 0 and add a short filter envelope.
  • Lead too static? A little filter envelope makes each note speak.

On the FM-1

  1. Every preset’s Envelope starts off. Hold ENV for about a second to switch it on; the FM-1 shows “Envelope On”, and the ENV button stays lit. The setting is saved with each preset.
  2. Press ENV to open Attack, Decay, Sustain and Release.
  3. On a freshly erased preset all four start at 0. Raise Sustain first, or every note will be a short blip.
  4. The filter envelope lives in the Filter group under EDIT: Envelope, Decay and Shape. On a blank VA preset, KNOB3 and KNOB4 are already Filter Env and Filter Decay.
  5. Connect the FM-1’s audio and the middle picture above draws its loudness in purple next to the page’s. Play the same note on both with Play on: Both and compare envelopes.

On FM presets, Release works differently: each FM operator has its own envelope, and the preset’s Release can only cut a fade short, never lengthen it. Lesson 8 covers why.

Where it comes from

1965
Composer Vladimir Ussachevsky asks Robert Moog for an envelope with four stages. Moog builds it as the 911 envelope module.
1970s
ARP Instruments labels the four stages Attack, Decay, Sustain and Release on its synthesizers, and “ADSR” becomes the standard name.
